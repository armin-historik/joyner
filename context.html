Marjorie Stewart Joyner · Historical research

Historical Context

Joyner's work in the broader development of twentieth-century permanent waving.

Joyner's 1928 permanent-waving apparatus was a significant contribution to American beauty technology. It should be placed within the longer history of thermal and chemical hair waving, not mistaken for the invention of permanent waving itself.

Permanent waving before Joyner

Charles Nessler demonstrated an early electrically heated permanent-wave system in London in 1906, following development work in the preceding years. By the 1920s, permanent waving had become an evolving field with competing methods, heating arrangements and winding techniques. Joyner entered an existing technological landscape.

What Joyner sought to improve

Her patent, US 1,693,515, described a machine designed to support and operate multiple hair-waving elements at the same time. Its claims and drawings show the apparatus as a specific mechanical arrangement. The technical record should be used to understand the claimed design; retrospective accounts should be identified as such when discussing inspiration or salon reception.

A distinct professional setting

Joyner's work emerged from the beauty-school and salon networks of the United States, particularly the Walker organisation. The history is not only one of apparatus design, but also of education, Black women's entrepreneurship, access to professional training and industry standards.

What remains to be established

The patent file documents invention and prosecution, not the total number of devices manufactured or their commercial diffusion. A second Joyner patent for a scalp protector is reported by the National Archives and National Inventors Hall of Fame; its patent number and complete file should be verified separately before being entered into the technical archive.

For a close reading of the surviving documents, visit Patent & Drawings. For Joyner's career, see Biography.

Research revision · 3 November 2026

Salons as institutions of community life

The Smithsonian's Black Main Street exhibition places Joyner in the wider history of Black-owned businesses. Salon ownership could provide economic autonomy where discriminatory credit and employment practices restricted other opportunities. Salons also functioned as places where information circulated and communities organised. The Smithsonian documents how profits from beauty businesses helped support educational institutions and civil-rights activity.

This social and economic history complements, but is distinct from, the technical history documented in Joyner's two patents.
